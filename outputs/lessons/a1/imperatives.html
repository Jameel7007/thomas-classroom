The system: the base verb does everything

An imperative starts with the base verb and has no written subject. Don’t makes it negative, please makes it polite, and let’s turns it into a shared plan.

purposepatternexample
positive instructionbase verb, no subjectTurn left at the light.
negativedon’t + base verbDon’t park here.
politeplease at the start or the endPlease wait here. Wait here, please.
suggestion with uslet’s + base verbLet’s take a break.

The verb never changes. No -s, no -ing, and no to: say Don’t worry, not Don’t to worry.

Get to the root: the hidden subject is always you

An imperative begins with the base verb because the subject you is understood from the situation. The speaker is directing the words at the listener: (You) open the file. English leaves you unspoken because it is already obvious. This is also why there is never a -s ending: she opens agrees with she, but an imperative always speaks to you, so there is nothing for the verb to agree with.

Why context is essential

Turn left. can be helpful directions. Take one tablet with food. can be medical instructions. Don’t move. can be a warning. The grammar is the same; purpose, relationship, and tone determine how the listener understands it.

The base verb makes imperatives direct and efficient

There is no tense ending, third-person -s, or auxiliary in a positive imperative: Wait. Be careful. Have a seat. For the negative, don’t carries the negative grammar, even with be: Don’t be late.

meaningformexample
do the actionbase verbSave the document.
do not do the actiondon’t + base verbDon’t delete the original.
emphasizedo + base verbDo come in. Do be careful.

Grammar is simple; social meaning is not

A bare imperative can sound neutral, friendly, urgent, or rude. The relationship and situation decide. Instructions on a screen (Enter your password) are neutral. The same direct form spoken to a coworker may need softening.

formtypical effectexample

please + imperativepolite but still directPlease send me the address.

When imperatives are friendly

Imperatives are normal and warm for directions, instructions, offers, and encouragement: Take a seat. Have some coffee. Don’t worry. Nobody hears these as orders.

For a request to a stranger or your boss, soften it: add please, or turn it into a question with Can you…? from the can lesson. Can you send me the file, please?

Please can go at the beginning or end. Voice and facial expression still matter; one polite word cannot repair an angry tone.

Let’s: the imperative for shared plans

Let’s + base verb suggests a plan that includes the speaker and the listener together: Let’s take a break. Let’s meet at the café at noon. The listener can agree or suggest something else, so it feels like an invitation, not a command.

Useful special forms

Let me… offers the speaker’s action: Let me help you. Let’s not… makes a negative shared suggestion: Let’s not be late. Adding you is unusual and often emphatic or contrastive: You wait here; Marco, come with me.

Build clear procedures

Good instructions divide a process into manageable actions and show the order. Use sequencing words such as first, next, then, after that, finally: First, open the app. Then select “Settings.” Finally, save your changes. Keep one main action in each step when clarity matters.

Use it in real life

Directions to your door: Guide the teacher from the nearest bus stop or metro station to your front door. Turn left… Go straight… Don’t take the first entrance.
In the kitchen: Teach a dish you really cook, step by step. First, chop the onions. Then add the rice. Don’t cover the pan yet.
Advice for a new student: Give four pieces of advice to someone starting English lessons. Don’t worry about mistakes. Ask questions. Practice a little every day.
Phone help: Explain how to do one thing on a phone: connect to Wi-Fi, change the language, or share a photo. Open Settings. Tap… Don’t forget to save.
Make a plan with let’s: Plan a free afternoon with a friend. Make three suggestions. Let’s meet at noon. Let’s try the new café. Let’s not stay out late.
