The system: have got and has got

Use have got with I / you / we / they. Use has got with he / she / it.

I / you / we / theyhe / she / it
positiveWe have got a car.She has got a car.
negativeWe haven’t got one.She hasn’t got one.
questionHave you got one?Has she got one?

Short answers: Yes, I have. / No, she hasn’t. Do not repeat got in a short answer.

Get to the root: a present connection

Have got describes a connection that is true now: something belongs to you, is part of your life, or is one of your characteristics. In I’ve got a bicycle, got does not mean “received” and does not make the sentence past. The whole chunk means “I have a bicycle now.”

kind of connectionexamplemeaning
possessionI’ve got a laptop.The laptop belongs to me.
relationshipShe’s got two brothers.They are part of her family.
featureThe apartment has got a balcony.The balcony is part of it.
condition / needI’ve got a headache. We’ve got a problem.This is our present situation.

Why have and has do the grammar work

In this structure, have / has behaves like an auxiliary. It agrees with the subject, moves to the front for questions, and combines directly with not. Got stays unchanged.

Follow the moving part

She has got a car. → Has she got a car? → She hasn’t got a car.

Only has moves or becomes negative. This is why Does she has got…? mixes two different systems and is not the target form.

Have got and have

For possession, both forms are correct. Have got is especially common in British English and in conversational English generally. American English often prefers ordinary have.

have got systemordinary have system
I’ve got a car.I have a car.
Have you got a car?Do you have a car?
I haven’t got a car.I don’t have a car.

Choose one system inside a sentence. Do not combine them as Do you have got…? Both systems communicate the same possession meaning.

Contractions make the pattern sound natural

In speech, positive forms are usually contracted: I’ve got, you’ve got, he’s got, we’ve got. Here he’s means he has, not he is, because it is followed by got. Negative forms commonly contract too: haven’t got, hasn’t got.

A useful boundary

Use have got mainly for possession and present connections, not for repeated actions. Say I have lunch at one, not normally I’ve got lunch at one, when you mean “eat lunch.”

Use it in real life

Pocket and bag check: Say five true things you have or haven’t got with you right now. I’ve got my phone. I haven’t got cash.
Ask the teacher: Ask five Have you got…? questions. Listen and give a short answer back. Have you got any pets? Have you got a car?
People: Describe a real person’s family, home, or useful possessions with he’s got or she’s got.
Not at home: Say three things your home hasn’t got. My apartment hasn’t got a balcony.
Same and different: Compare yourself with a friend or coworker. We’ve both got laptops. She’s got a car, but I haven’t.
