Use an object pronoun after a verb or preposition: see her, for him, with them.

Get to the root: roles inside the sentence

A pronoun changes form because its role changes. The subject is the grammatical starting point—the person or thing connected to the verb before it. An object receives, experiences, or completes the action, or follows a preposition.

Same people, different roles

She calls me. I call her.

The two people have not changed, but their sentence roles have. In the first sentence, she starts the action and me receives it. In the second, those roles reverse.

Pronouns are reference tools

A pronoun lets the listener recover a person or thing without repeating the full noun. The reference must be clear. I spoke to Maya and sent her the file is clear because her points back to Maya. If several women were mentioned, repeating the name may be clearer than using her.

Number and person must match the reference: one male person → him; one female person → her; one thing or animal when sex is not relevant → it; multiple people or things → them.

Object pronouns with common verb patterns

patternexample
verb + objectCall me. I know him.
verb + object + thingSend her the link. Tell us the time.
verb + thing + to + objectSend the link to her. Give it to them.
preposition + objectWait for me. Sit next to him.

It, them, and one

Use it/them when referring to the same known thing or things: Where is my key? I need it. Use one/ones when you mean another example of that type: I need a key. Do you have one?

In careful standard usage, say between you and me, because a pronoun after a preposition takes the object form.

Use it in real life

People around you: Name three people, then say how often you call, see, or help them.
Office handoff: Give instructions using him, her, it, us, and them.
Quick replies: Respond naturally: “Who has the keys?” “Who can help?” “Do you know Sara?”
