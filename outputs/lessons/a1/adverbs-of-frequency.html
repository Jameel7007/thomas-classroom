The system

verbpositionexample
most verbsbefore the main verbI usually walk.

sometimesalso possible at the startSometimes we eat out.

Never already has a negative meaning: say I never drive, not I don’t never drive.

Get to the root: turn repeated events into a pattern

Frequency adverbs help the listener build a picture from many occasions. Imagine looking at every Monday for a year. If the same event appears on every Monday, it always happens. If it appears on no Mondays, it never happens. The words between those ends describe less exact patterns.

frequencybasic ideaexample
alwaysevery relevant occasionI always check the address.
usuallynormal pattern; exceptions existWe usually finish at five.
oftenmany occasionsShe often travels for work.
sometimessome occasions, not mostThey sometimes eat out.
rarely / hardly eververy few occasionsHe hardly ever drives.
neverno occasionsI never work on Sundays.

The percentages in learning diagrams are only memory aids. Usually is not a mathematical 80%, and different speakers may choose different words for the same real pattern.

Why word order changes with be

With most verbs, put the frequency adverb before the main action: I usually cook. With be, the verb is short and structurally central, so the adverb follows it: I am usually tired. In longer verb phrases, it normally follows the first auxiliary: She can always help.

A practical positioning test

  • Find the main verb.
  • If it is a form of be, place the adverb after it.
  • If it is another simple-present verb, place the adverb before it.
  • Keep a time phrase such as every Friday near the end.

Frequency adverbs and frequency phrases

These two tools answer the same broad question, but they give different kinds of information.

toolpositioninformation
adverbusually inside the verb phraseI often call her. A general pattern.
frequency phraseusually at the endI call her twice a week. A measurable rate.
every + timeusually at the endI call her every Sunday. A repeated schedule.

You can combine them when the meaning is sensible: I usually exercise three times a week. The adverb describes how normal the pattern is; the phrase measures the pattern.

Ask with How often…?

Use How often + do/does + subject + base verb?: How often do you cook? With be, move be directly: How often are you late? Answers can be general (Hardly ever) or exact (About once a month).

Meaning traps

Never, rarely, and hardly ever already carry a negative or near-negative idea. Standard English says I never go, not I don’t never go. Also distinguish hard (“with effort”) from hardly ever (“almost never”).
