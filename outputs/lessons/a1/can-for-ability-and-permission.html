Can never takes -s: say He can drive, not he cans drive.

Get to the root: possibility around an action

Can is a modal verb. A modal does not name the main action. It adds the speaker’s view of that action—in this lesson, whether the action is possible because of skill, circumstances, or permission.

One form, related meanings

  • I can swim. My ability makes swimming possible.
  • We can meet at six. The circumstances make the meeting possible.
  • You can leave now. Permission makes leaving possible.
  • Can I sit here? I am asking whether permission makes it possible.

Why the base verb never changes

Can carries the grammar for the complete verb phrase, so the action verb stays in its simplest form. There is no to, no third-person -s, and no do/does.

That makes modals efficient: He can work. Can he work? He can’t work. You change or move can; work remains stable.

Meaning depends on the situation

The words alone do not always tell you whether can means ability or permission. Context does.

sentencelikely contextmeaning
Can you drive?choosing a driverDo you have the ability?
Can you drive the company car?asking about a workplace ruleDo you have permission?
Can you help me?a request to another personWill you help, if possible?

Permission and politeness

Can I…? is normal and friendly for everyday permission. Can you…? is often a request, not a question about skill. Tone and please make the request warmer: Can you open the window, please? A positive answer is usually Of course or Sure, not only Yes, I can.

Hear the strong and weak forms

In a normal positive sentence, can is often weak and unstressed: I can SWIM. In can’t, the negative is important and is normally clearer or stronger. Listen carefully because this sound difference changes the meaning completely.
