Common verbs & adjectives

The most useful words you own are the everyday ones. Learn high-frequency verbs as chunks — make a decision, do the dishes, take a break, have lunch — and pair common adjectives with their opposites: cheap / expensive, busy / free, easy / difficult.

In English, the verb and the noun travel together as a collocation. You can't always translate them word for word: we say do homework and make dinner, not the reverse. Learn the whole chunk and you'll sound natural.

Notice before the rule

Read about Marco's Monday

"In the morning I make coffee and have breakfast. I take the bus to work. At noon I have a meeting, then I take a short break. After work I do the dishes, do some exercise, and make dinner."

  • Four verbs do a lot of work here: make, do, take, and have. Which nouns go with each one?
  • We say make dinner but do the dishes. Both are about food, so the choice isn't about meaning — it's a fixed pair. What helps you remember each one?
  • Look at take the bus and take a break. Can you think of one more thing you take every day?

The system: make · do · take · have

These four verbs each carry a small family of fixed chunks. Learn the noun with its verb:

verbcommon chunks
makemake a mistake  ·  make dinner  ·  make a decision
dodo homework  ·  do the dishes  ·  do exercise
taketake a break  ·  take a shower  ·  take the bus  ·  take a photo
havehave lunch  ·  have a meeting  ·  have a good time

Meaning check

  • What's the difference between make and do? Roughly, make = create or produce a result (make dinner, make a decision); do = an action, a task, or work (do homework, do the dishes). When unsure, learn the chunk by heart.
  • Why take the bus, not go the bus? With transport we usually say take: take the bus, take a taxi, take the train. We say go by bus only with by.
  • Is it have lunch or eat lunch? Both work, but have sounds more natural for meals: have breakfast, have lunch, have dinner.

Useful adjectives — in opposite pairs

Adjectives are easiest to remember two at a time, as opposites. Put the adjective before the noun, or after be:

a difficult exam   ·   The exam is difficult.

A few useful adjectives have no everyday opposite at this level — just learn them as they are: important, beautiful.

Use it in real life

Your morning routine: Say four true sentences about your morning using these verbs. I make coffee. I take a shower. I have breakfast. I take the bus to work.
Your day at work or study: Tell me about today. I have a meeting at ten. I take a break at noon. I do a lot of work in the afternoon.
Describe three things: Use the adjectives for things around you. My phone is expensive. My job is interesting. Today is very busy.
Agree or disagree: I say a sentence; you answer with an opposite adjective. "Math is easy." → "For me, math is difficult."
Plan together: We make a simple plan for the weekend using the chunks. On Saturday let's have lunch downtown, then take some photos and do some exercise.

Reflect

Say the chunks back

Complete this aloud: “I do my ______. I make a ______. I take a ______. I have ______. The opposite of cheap is ______; the opposite of busy is ______.”

Then say two true sentences about your day with these verbs, and one sentence describing something with an adjective.

Nicely done — this is the last A1 vocabulary lesson. You now have a working set of everyday verbs and adjectives to build real sentences. Carry these chunks into every conversation.
