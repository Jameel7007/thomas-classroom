Comparatives & superlatives

Compare people, places, and choices. Build clear sentences like The market is cheaper than the supermarket, She is the best cook in our family, and This café is as good as the other one.

≈ 55–65 min comparing places and choices cheaper · the cheapest than · the … in 11 interactive tasks

Comparatives put two things side by side. Superlatives pick the number one in a group. This is everyday grammar for choices, opinions, and recommendations.

The system: count the syllables

Short adjectives change their ending. Long adjectives stay the same and take more or the most in front.

adjective typecomparativesuperlative
short: old, cheap, fastolder, cheaper, fasterthe oldest, the cheapest, the fastest
ends in -y: busy, happy, easybusier, happier, easierthe busiest, the happiest, the easiest
one vowel + one consonant: big, hotbigger, hotterthe biggest, the hottest
long: expensive, comfortable, interestingmore expensive, more comfortable, more interestingthe most expensive, the most comfortable, the most interesting

Spelling helpers: -y becomes -ier and -iest. A short vowel plus one consonant doubles the consonant: big → bigger, hot → hottest.

Get to the root: one scale, two jobs

Both forms put things on the same scale of cheap, big, or busy. A comparative points at the difference between two things, so it needs than to name the second thing. A superlative points at number one in a group, so it needs the, because a group has only one top item. The group often appears after in or of: the tallest building in the city, the best day of the trip.

jobformframeexample
compare two thingscheaper / more expensive+ thanThe bus is cheaper than the taxi.
pick number onethe cheapest / the most expensivethe … in / ofIt is the cheapest way to travel in this city.

Follow the frame

The market is cheaper than the supermarket. → The market is the cheapest place in town.

When you hear than, two things are being compared. When you hear the … in or the … of, someone is pointing at number one in a group.

Irregulars: good, bad, and far

Three very common adjectives break the system completely. Memorize them as chunks.

adjectivecomparativesuperlative
goodbetterthe best
badworsethe worst
farfartherthe farthest

Say better, never gooder or more good. You will also hear further; for physical distance, farther is the safe American English choice.

Equal things: as … as

When two things sit at the same point on the scale, keep the base adjective and frame it with as … as: This café is as good as the other one. The negative means less: The bus is not as fast as the metro. No -er, no more, no than.

The classic error: more better

A comparative needs only one signal. -er is a signal. More is a signal. Never use both together.

say thisnot this
cheapermore cheaper
bettermore better
easiermore easier

Use it in real life

Your city: Compare two neighborhoods, cafés, or supermarkets you really know. The center is busier than my neighborhood. The market is cheaper than the supermarket.
Ask the teacher: Ask three questions with a comparative or a superlative. Listen and react. Which is cheaper in your city, the bus or the metro? What is the best restaurant near your home?
Then and now: Compare your current home or job with an old one. Use was and were. My old apartment was smaller, but the rent was cheaper.
Number one people: Say three superlative sentences about your family, friends, or coworkers. My brother is the tallest person in my family. Ana is the most patient person on our team.
Same and different: Use as … as and not as … as about your life. My street is as quiet as yours. My cooking is not as good as my mother’s.
