Present perfect have / has + past participle

Talk about your life experience up to now: I’ve been to Peru, Have you ever worked abroad?, She’s just left, and I haven’t finished yet.

The present perfect connects your past to right now. Use it for experience, news, and things finished or not finished yet, when the time is not stated or not over.

The system: have / has + past participle

Use have with I / you / we / they. Use has with he / she / it. Then add the past participle of the main verb.

I / you / we / theyhe / she / it
positiveI’ve worked abroad.She’s worked abroad.
negativeWe haven’t finished.He hasn’t finished.
questionHave you finished?Has she finished?

Short answers: Yes, I have. / No, she hasn’t. Do not repeat the participle in a short answer.

Follow the moving part

She has finished. → Has she finished? → She hasn’t finished.

Only has moves to the front or takes not. You already know this move from have got: Has she got a car? She hasn’t got time. The participle never changes, and you never need do or does in this system.

Get to the root: experience up to now

When you say I’ve been to Japan, you are not telling a story about one moment. You are describing your life up to now: at some point before now, this happened, and it is part of your experience today. The exact time is not stated because the time is not the point.

useexamplemeaning
life experienceI’ve been to Japan twice.At some time before now. The time is not stated.
question with everHave you ever been to Japan?At any time in your life until now?
negative with neverI’ve never eaten sushi.At no time in my life until now.

Use ever in questions and never in negative statements. Never already makes the sentence negative, so the verb stays positive: I’ve never eaten, not I haven’t never eaten.

just, already, and yet

Three small words tell your listener how new or how expected something is. Two go inside the verb; one goes at the end.

wordmeaningpositionexample
justa moment agobetween have / has and the participleI’ve just finished the call.
alreadysooner than expectedbetween have / has and the participleShe’s already left.
yetuntil now, in questions and negativesat the endHave you eaten yet? I haven’t called yet.

Present perfect or past simple?

The one contrast that matters

I’ve been to Peru.  (my experience up to now; no time stated)
I went to Peru in 2019.  (one finished moment; the time is stated)

The moment you name a finished time, English switches to the past simple. Say I saw it yesterday, not I’ve seen it yesterday.

Finished-time words force the past simple: yesterday, last week, last year, in 2019, two years ago, when I was a child.

been or gone?

She’s gone to lunch. She left and is still out. She’s been to Japan. She went and came back; it is her experience now. Use been for experience and gone for someone who has not come back.

Use it in real life

Your experience list: Say five true sentences about your life with I’ve and I’ve never. I’ve worked in two cities. I’ve never eaten oysters.
Ask the teacher: Ask three Have you ever…? questions. When the answer is yes, follow up in the past simple. Have you ever been to Asia? When did you go?
Today so far: Describe your day with just, already, and yet. I’ve already had two coffees. I haven’t had lunch yet.
Someone you know: Describe a friend’s or coworker’s experiences with he’s or she’s. My sister has been to ten countries. She’s never visited me here.
Two-sentence stories: Open with an experience, then give the finished-time detail. I’ve been to Istanbul. I went there in 2022.
