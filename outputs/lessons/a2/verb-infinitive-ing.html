When one verb follows another, the first verb decides the shape of the second: want to go, but enjoy going. Speakers store these shapes as chunks, and this lesson helps you do the same.

The system: two verb families

Some first verbs are followed by to + base verb. Others are followed by the -ing form. Learn each verb together with its pattern.

Get to the root: the first verb decides

Why do we say want to go but enjoy going? The second verb does not choose its own shape. The first verb controls it. This information belongs to the first verb, so we learn it with the verb, not as a separate rule.

There is still a helpful tendency. Verbs that point forward to a goal usually take to: you want to, hope to, plan to, and decide to do things you have not done yet, and to works like an arrow toward that action. Verbs about the activity itself usually take -ing: you enjoy, finish, keep, or practice an activity that is real and present, and the -ing form names that activity like a thing.

Feel the arrow

I want to swim. The swim is still ahead of you, a goal.

I enjoy swimming. The swimming is the experience itself, already part of your life.

Learn the chunk, not the rule

Store the pattern inside the verb: want to go, need to buy, hope to find, enjoy working, keep practicing, don’t mind waiting. This course teaches chunks like there is and do you for the same reason: your memory holds whole pieces better than abstract rules. When you meet a new verb, learn one full sentence with it, and the pattern comes for free.

Both forms, same meaning: like, love, hate, start

A small group of verbs accepts both patterns with no real change in meaning. Choose either form. American English often prefers to after these verbs, but both are natural.

verb-ing formto + verb
likeI like cooking.I like to cook.
loveShe loves running.She loves to run.
hateI hate waiting.I hate to wait.
startIt started raining.It started to rain.

Would like: the polite want

Would like is the polite version of want, and it always takes to + verb: I’d like to book a table for eight. I’d like to change my appointment. You met this politeness at A1 when ordering food; now you can attach any verb to it.

Careful: like can take both forms, but would like only takes to + verb. Say I’d like to cook, never I’d like cooking.

After a preposition, always -ing

One dependable rule: after a preposition, a verb takes the -ing form. She’s good at cooking. I’m interested in learning Japanese. Thanks for waiting.

Use it in real life

Your free time: Say five true sentences about your hobbies with enjoy, love, and like. I enjoy walking in the evening. I love cooking for friends.
Your goals: Say three true sentences about this year with want to, hope to, and plan to. I want to improve my English. I plan to visit my sister.
Ask the teacher: Ask three questions with these patterns, then ask one follow-up. Do you enjoy teaching online? What do you want to learn next?
Polite requests: You are at a restaurant or a hotel. Make three requests with I’d like to. I’d like to book a table for eight. I’d like to pay by card.
Habits, good and bad: Say two true things with keep and two with don’t mind. I keep forgetting to drink water. I don’t mind working late on Thursdays.
