Vocabulary · Lesson 10

The alphabet & spelling

You already know the 26 letters by shape. Now learn their names out loud — so you can spell your last name on a call, read out your email, or confirm a city on a form. The name of a letter is not always the sound it makes in a word.

Notice before the rule

The name of the letter, not the sound

Read each letter name aloud, then read the word next to it:

H — say "aitch" — but in hotel it sounds /h/.
W — say "double-u" — but in work it sounds /w/.
C — say "see" — but in city it sounds /s/, and in café it sounds /k/.

  • When you spell a word out loud, do you say the letter name or the sound? Say the name.
  • Is the name of a letter always the same as its sound in a word?
  • When in real life do you need to spell something letter by letter?

The system: vowels, consonants, and rhyming groups

The five main vowel letters are A, E, I, O, U. The letter Y can also represent a vowel sound, as in my and gym. Every English syllable has a vowel sound.

Many letter names rhyme. Grouping them makes them easier to say and to hear on a call:

Spelling out loud — the moves you need

  • Two of the same letter together: double. Miller → M-I-double-L-E-R.
  • A big letter at the start of a name: capital. A small letter: lowercase. capital A, lowercase n.
  • In an email: @ = "at", . = "dot", _ = "underscore". ana_lopez@mail.com → ana underscore lopez at mail dot com.
  • Easy to confuse on a call: B / V, E / I, G / J, M / N. Slow down and say a clue word: "B for Boston."

Read and choose

Spelling on the phone

“Can you spell your last name, please?” “Yes. It’s Reyes: R-E-Y-E-S.” “Thank you. And your first name?” “Marco: M-A-R-C-O.” “Perfect. Your email is marco@fastmail.com, right?” “Yes, that’s right.”

Read, then choose

1. What is the last name?

2. How many letters are in the first name?

3. What does the person check at the end?

Use it in real life

Your name on a call: Say your first name, then spell it letter by letter. Then do your last name. Use a clue word if a letter is hard: "M for Mike."
Your city: Say where you live, then spell it slowly. The teacher writes it down and reads it back. Did it match?
Your email: Read your email aloud using at, dot, and underscore. The teacher types it. Confirm: "Yes, that's right."
Filling a form: Role-play a front desk. One person asks "Can you spell your last name?" and "How do you spell that?"; the other answers. Then switch.
Information gap: The teacher spells a city or name out loud once. The student writes it, then reads it back to confirm. Repeat with harder words until both match.
