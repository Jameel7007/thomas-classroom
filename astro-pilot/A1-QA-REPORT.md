## Present continuous: issues found and resolved

- The original explanation of an action “viewed from inside” was strong and was
  preserved as the organizing meaning. The opening now connects exact present,
  temporary around-now use, and a clearly arranged future to that same open
  viewpoint.
- Future examples such as `this Friday` and `tonight` previously appeared in
  practice before the use was explained. A compact boundary now requires an
  organized arrangement and visible future-time context.
- The consonant-doubling description was too broad. The reference now includes
  one-syllable and final-stress conditions, non-doubling `visiting`, `opening`,
  `snowing`, and `fixing`, plus `being` and `seeing` outside the silent-e row.
- The former state-verb note risked becoming a memorized blacklist. Contrasts
  now show how meaning changes the pattern: possession versus `having lunch`,
  opinion versus `thinking about`, and appearance versus `looking at`.
- Existing closed items now state the routine, live observation, temporary
  schedule, diagnostic result, or confirmed arrangement that makes one tense
  and polarity defensible. Ambiguous final-quiz choices were replaced.
- The short-answer item no longer accepts both `isn't` and `isn't working`; it
  explicitly requests one word. The movement question now contrasts a live
  trip with a grammatical routine question and a different location question.
- All 49 scored decisions provide a contextual first hint, exact repair, and
  concept-specific correct feedback. Typed inputs have meaningful accessible
  labels rather than numbered labels.
- Spoken guidance preserves contractions, sentence stress, linking, and the
  final nasal sound in `-ing`. A closed tutor-read check distinguishes routine,
  temporary, state, activity, current question, and future arrangement before
  its transcript is opened.
- Production no longer requires a camera, room, street, screen, live location,
  schedule, family, friends, or messages. Public labeled scenes, teacher-read
  text cards, and invented profiles provide equivalent access.
- The final two-time update requires a routine, two continuous statements, a
  negative, a question, a state/action decision, and a timed arrangement. The
  next-day route retrieves the core tense and meaning decisions before
  rereading.

## Hobbies and free time: issues found and resolved

- The original page presented `go + -ing` as the rule for activities ending in
  `-ing`. That overgenerated phrases such as `go reading`. The revision treats
  `go hiking`, `go swimming`, and similar forms as established outing or
  movement chunks while teaching independent verbs such as `read`, `watch`,
  `listen to`, `take photos`, `cook`, and `garden`.
- `Play`, `go`, and `do` remain useful tendencies, but the learner now stores a
  complete activity chunk rather than deriving it from an unreliable category.
  `Do exercise` was replaced with natural U.S. options such as `exercise`,
  `work out`, `do a workout`, and `do some exercises`.
- The earlier lesson incorrectly said that `like`, `love`, and `enjoy` all
  require an `-ing` form. The revision keeps the mandatory `enjoy + -ing`
  pattern while explaining that ordinary `like` and `love` also allow `to +
  verb`, often with overlapping preference meanings.
- One tennis contrast now separates five messages: present-simple routine,
  preference with `like + -ing`, experienced enjoyment with `enjoy + -ing`,
  ability with `can + base verb`, and present interest with `would like + to +
  base verb`.
- Question choice is now purpose-driven. `What do you do in your free time?`
  asks about activities, `What do you like doing?` asks about preferences, `How
  often...?` asks about frequency, `Can you...?` asks about ability, and `Are
  you free this Saturday?` asks about availability rather than hobbies.
- Small-word and U.S. usage guidance covers `play soccer`, `play the guitar`,
  `watch a movie`, `watch TV`, `listen to music`, `take photos`, `ride a bike`,
  `on weekends`, `on the weekend`, and zero-preposition `this weekend`.
- The `-ing` spelling system retrieves ordinary addition, final silent-e
  deletion, and short-vowel consonant doubling without pretending the spelling
  determines which activity verb a chunk takes.
- All 49 scored decisions provide a fictional profile, schedule, stated
  meaning, question purpose, or exact activity listing. Each includes a
  contextual first hint, exact repair, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Spoken guidance covers light `do you`, the common `whaddaya` listening form,
  linked activity chunks, nasal `-ing`, word stress, and /s/, /z/, and /ɪz/
  third-person endings. A six-item tutor-read check contrasts routine, ability,
  preference, present interest, activity inquiry, and availability before its
  script opens.
- Personal hobby, ability, health, body, schedule, work, family, friend,
  location, financial, religious, device, travel, and weekend disclosures are
  no longer required. A fictional community-center guide offers indoor,
  outdoor, online, quiet, social, low-equipment, and flexible-movement options.
- The final 45-to-60-second recommendation requires two complete activity
  chunks, stated visitor evidence, one schedule or access detail, a follow-up
  question, and a polite invitation. Next-day retrieval then checks an
  independent activity verb, `enjoy + -ing`, and availability.

## Clothes and shopping: issues found and resolved

- The original page grouped shoes and jeans under one “pair or many” rule. The
  revision separates ordinary plural nouns such as `shoes`, where `a shoe` can
  name one physical item, from plural-form garments such as `pants`, `jeans`,
  and `shorts`, where one complete garment still takes plural grammar.
- `Clothes` and `clothing` now have an explicit countability and agreement
  boundary. `Clothes` is plural but not countable, so the learner counts shirts,
  garments, or items of clothing. `Clothing` is uncountable and takes a singular
  verb.
- The head noun now controls pair agreement: `these jeans are`, `this pair of
  jeans is`, and `two pairs of jeans are`. A later pronoun can follow the chosen
  viewpoint: `the jeans ... them` or `the pair ... it`.
- The former near-only `this / these` system now retrieves the complete
  near/far contrast with `this / that / these / those`. Each scene carries a
  consistent chain through noun number, `is / are`, and later `it / them`.
- Four clothing verbs now have separate meanings. `Wear` presents the state,
  `put on` moves clothing onto the body, `take off` removes it, and `try on`
  tests it before a decision. The separable-verb boundary requires `try it on`
  and `try them on`, not `try on it` or `try on them`.
- Shopping language now distinguishes size labels from physical fit. It includes
  singular and plural forms for asking about another size, trying on, reporting
  `too tight / loose / short / long`, asking price, buying, and declining without
  inventing a reason.
- `On sale` is explicitly discounted in common U.S. shopping English, while
  `for sale` only means available to buy. The lesson also clarifies U.S. `pants`,
  British `trousers`, U.S. `sweater`, British `jumper`, `sneakers`, `tennis
  shoes`, `purse`, `handbag`, and two natural readings of a `.99` price.
- All 49 scored decisions provide a visible store scene, noun viewpoint, stated
  purpose, price, size, fit, or catalog fact. Each has a contextual first hint,
  exact repair, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Spoken guidance covers one-syllable `clothes`, two-syllable `clothing`, voiced
  demonstratives, plural /s/, /z/, and /ɪz/, linked `Can I try it on?`, and
  one-syllable `I’ll`. A six-item tutor-read check contrasts number, distance,
  `it / them`, and `on sale / for sale` before its script opens.
- Personal clothing, body, size, purchase, budget, brand, workplace, religious,
  health, mobility, travel, family, gender, and appearance disclosures are no
  longer required. Fictional catalogs and customer cards provide the evidence,
  include a step-free fitting-room detail, and preserve a no-buy response.
- The final 45-to-60-second exchange requires a singular and plural-form item,
  size or color, try-on pronoun, fit, price, sale meaning, and a buy-or-decline
  ending. The linked next-day route retrieves clothes agreement, pair agreement,
  plural reference, and pronoun placement before rereading.

## Weather and seasons: issues found and resolved

- The original page moved directly from current weather to typical seasonal
  weather without separating weather from climate. The revision defines weather
  as short-term conditions and climate as patterns observed over many years, so
  one day and one long-term pattern cannot substitute for each other.
- `Rainy` and `raining`, and `snowy` and `snowing`, were presented as parallel
  forms without a defensible viewpoint. The new live-camera sequence contrasts
  a broader day or place description with an event visibly in progress at an
  exact time.
- The revised word-family table distinguishes nouns, adjectives, and events for
  rain, snow, wind, sun, cloud, and fog. It blocks invented forms such as `it’s
  sunning`, `it’s winding`, and `it’s clouding`, and supplies natural real-subject
  frames such as `The sun is shining` and `The wind is blowing`.
- Weather `it` is now explained as a required grammar subject rather than an
  object reference. `Lightning` and `thunder` are introduced as normally
  uncountable in basic weather use, while `a rainbow` and `a storm` are countable.
- Question choice now follows the information requested. `What’s the weather
  like?` and `How’s the weather?` request a broad description, `Is it raining?`
  checks one event, `What’s the temperature?` requests a number and unit, and
  `What’s the forecast for Friday?` asks about expected later conditions.
- Temperature values no longer omit their measurement system. The page explains
  everyday U.S. Fahrenheit use, widespread Celsius use, singular `1 degree`,
  plural `72 degrees`, and forecast `high` versus `low` without requiring a
  conversion exercise.
- A percentage chance of rain is explicitly a probability estimate rather than
  a guarantee, duration, or percentage of a city that is currently wet. Closed
  items avoid claiming a more technical definition than the forecast card gives.
- The former season-matching task treated hot summer, snowy winter, rainy spring,
  and windy fall as universal. The revision makes the four-season model one
  model, contrasts Northern and Southern Hemisphere January, acknowledges wet
  and dry season systems, and refuses to infer conditions from a season name.
- Season time frames now distinguish `in spring` and U.S. `in the spring` from
  zero-preposition `this / next / last spring`. U.S. `fall` and especially
  British `autumn` remain explicitly related rather than treated as correctness
  and error.
- All 49 scored decisions include a live-camera time, forecast label, stated
  probability, unit, hemisphere, service card, or grammar frame. Each provides
  a contextual first hint, exact repair, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Spoken guidance covers one-syllable `it’s`, linked `What’s the weather like?`,
  voiced `th`, weather/whether homophony in common U.S. speech, reduced and
  careful `temperature`, `Fahrenheit`, `Celsius`, rain-family syllables, and the
  weather pronunciation of `wind`.
- A six-item tutor-read check contrasts broader condition, exact live event,
  forecast high, rain probability, and opposite hemisphere seasons before the
  transcript opens.
- Real location, weather, climate, home, clothing, health, mobility, work,
  travel, schedule, financial, family, and access disclosures are not required.
  Fictional service cards provide the evidence and clearly defer real severe-
  weather decisions to official local alerts.
- The final 45-to-60-second bulletin requires current condition, live event,
  later forecast, temperature unit, probability meaning, place-specific season,
  supplied service change, and follow-up question. Next-day retrieval checks the
  word family, hemisphere, and zero-preposition next-season frame.

## Common verbs and adjectives: issues found and resolved

- The original page displayed 19 isolated action verbs, but most practice used
  only `make`, `do`, `take`, and `have`. Each visual now carries a complete,
  usable chunk, and later work separates light-verb collocations from ordinary
  verb complements.
- `Make`, `do`, `take`, and `have` are presented through useful tendencies, not
  laws. `Make coffee` and `have coffee` demonstrate how the same noun can take
  a different verb when the speaker changes viewpoint.
- U.S. usage boundaries now cover `take / ride the bus`, `go by bus`, `take a
  shower`, natural exercise expressions, and `have / eat lunch`. The page no
  longer promotes bare `do exercise` as a neutral universal chunk.
- Ordinary verbs are organized by complement pattern: a verb that can stand
  alone, a direct object, a required preposition, or a meaning-dependent frame.
  Explicit contrasts include `listen to / hear`, `call the office`, `speak
  English / speak to a person`, and `buy a ticket / pay for a ticket`.
- Present-simple form remains visible around each chunk. Third-person positive
  forms carry the marker, while `does` and `doesn't` return the main verb to its
  base form.
- Adjective grammar now covers position before a noun and after `be`, invariant
  form with plural nouns, and questions formed by moving `be` rather than adding
  `do`.
- The false opposite pair `happy / tired` was removed. Relative meanings such
  as easy, difficult, interesting, boring, clear, and confusing now depend on
  evidence and viewpoint rather than automatic labels.
- `Cheap / inexpensive` and both senses of `free` receive explicit treatment.
  The thing-person contrasts `boring / bored`, `interesting / interested`, and
  `confusing / confused` are separated, and `very` is distinguished from `too`
  through a stated practical limit.
- All 49 scored decisions now have enough context for one defensible answer,
  a first hint that names the relevant distinction, an exact repair, and
  concept-specific correct feedback.
- Spoken guidance covers chunk stress, linking, third-person endings, reduced
  `doesn't` and `do you`, and adjective stress. A six-item tutor-read check
  tests preparation versus experience, both meanings of `free`, and
  thing-person adjective meaning before its transcript opens.
- Routine, work, study, purchases, budgets, transport, home, relationships,
  emotions, health, ability, and access disclosures are optional. Fictional
  community-center evidence supports the same language work without requiring
  personal information.
- The final 45-to-60-second shift briefing integrates all four light verbs,
  ordinary complement patterns, present-simple form, adjective position,
  polysemy, degree, and thing-person meaning. Next-day retrieval checks the
  highest-risk chunks and the single third-person marker.

## Rooms and furniture: issues found and resolved

- The original lesson treated nearly every thing in a room as furniture. The
  revision separates rooms, movable furniture, appliances, installed fixtures,
  small objects, and room features. It also makes the central countability rule
  explicit: `furniture` is uncountable, while individual nouns such as `chair`
  and `sofa` are countable.
- The earlier page used `there is / there are` mainly as a number formula. The
  revision connects form to information flow: `There is a lamp in the room`
  introduces a new object, while `It is on the desk` locates that known object.
  The plural chain uses `there are` followed by `they are`.
- `The room has...` now provides a distinct feature viewpoint. It is compared
  directly with existence language so learners can describe the same scene in
  two natural ways without treating one pattern as universally correct.
- Room-object matches no longer claim that furniture has one natural home. The
  matching drill refers to an explicit fictional floor plan, and the lesson
  acknowledges studios and shared spaces that combine room functions.
- Place language now distinguishes enclosure, surface contact, direct lower
  position, higher position without contact, immediate adjacency, general
  nearness, two-point position, and front-back relationships. `On the wall`
  contrasts with `above the sofa`, and `in front of` contrasts with `in the
  front of`.
- U.S. vocabulary guidance now covers conversational `couch`, `fridge`, and
  `TV`, plus the useful `bathroom / restroom` and `closet / wardrobe`
  boundaries. These are framed as common choices rather than universal rules.
- All 49 scored decisions include a complete fictional scene, information
  status, number, category, or spatial cue. Each provides a contextual first
  hint, exact repair, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Spoken guidance covers natural stress in room compounds, U.S. pronunciations
  of `furniture`, `comfortable`, and `refrigerator`, linked `there’s a`, light
  `there are`, reduced `next to`, and plural /s/, /z/, and /ɪz/ endings. A
  six-item tutor-read check distinguishes introductions, known locations, and
  vertical relationships before its script opens.
- Prompts no longer require a real home, bedroom, address, possessions, work
  setup, neighborhood, family, finances, photos, or location. The final
  45-to-60-second task presents a fictional guest studio and includes a
  language-only clear-path planning step without making a medical assessment.
- The linked next-day route retrieves uncountable `furniture`, known singular
  reference, and `above`, then repairs `There are some furniture` before the
  learner rereads the lesson.

## Daily routines and telling the time: issues found and resolved

- The original word bank implied one “normal day” and mixed actions with the
  noun `night`. It is now an optional routine bank rather than a lifestyle
  standard. `night` became `relax in the evening`, and the regionally ambiguous
  `wash up` became the clear U.S. routine chunk `wash my face`.
- The clock system previously implied that `o’clock`, `half past`, and
  `quarter past/to` covered most routine times. The revision presents common
  U.S. number style first (`six thirty`, `eight forty-five`) while preserving
  past/to style as correct and widely understood. It also teaches `oh` in
  `8:05`, a.m./p.m., noon, midnight, and the exact-hour limit on `o’clock`.
- `Wake up` and `get up` were previously listed without a meaning contrast.
  Learners now distinguish ending sleep from leaving the bed. Parallel
  contrasts separate `go to work` from `start work` and `finish work` from
  `get home`.
- Small-word boundaries are now explicit: `go to work` but `go home / get
  home`, general meal chunks without articles, possessive changes in `brush my
  teeth`, and singular `their` when a fictional profile does not specify
  gender.

## Prepositions of time and place: issues found and resolved

- The original point, surface, and container model was a strong organizing
  idea. The revision keeps it as a thinking tool but no longer presents physical
  size as a complete law; conventional chunks remain necessary evidence.
- Time frames still progress from `at 9:30` to `on Monday` to `in July`. The
  fixed U.S. chunks `at night` and `on the weekend` remain explicit, and the
  zero-preposition boundary now includes `today`, `tonight`, `tomorrow`, and
  `yesterday` alongside `this`, `next`, `last`, and `every`.
- Place frames retain points, surfaces or lines, and containing areas. Activity
  locations such as `at work` and `at school` are now described as a common
  functional viewpoint rather than a literal statement about building geometry.
- A missing address contrast now separates a general street line, `on Oak
  Street`, from an exact numbered point, `at 245 Oak Street`. Closed items state
  which of those two map pictures is intended.
- Common U.S. transportation chunks now distinguish `on a bus / train / plane`
  from `in a car / taxi` and explicitly identify them as conventions rather than
  a perfect physical rule.
- Close noun contrasts now separate `at school / in school`, `in bed / on the
  bed`, and `on time / in time`. The existing `at the hospital / in the hospital`
  distinction remains as an additional purpose-versus-inside contrast.
- All 49 scored decisions identify a timetable, calendar, map, inventory,
  address, activity location, vehicle, or intended spatial relation and provide
  one contextual hint plus an exact repair. Typed inputs have descriptive
  accessible labels.
- Spoken guidance now covers light prepositions, forward linking in `at-eight`,
  `on-Oak Street`, and `in-August`, reduced unstressed `at`, and contrastive
  stress when the preposition changes the intended frame.
- A six-item tutor-read check tests exact time, named day, month, numbered
  address, public transportation, and a small vehicle before its transcript is
  opened.
- Production no longer requests a learner’s real address, home, belongings,
  schedule, workplace, school, travel, family, or live location. Fictional maps,

- The final community-event plan requires time and place scales, street and
  address contrast, transportation, a zero-preposition time expression, and a

## Object pronouns: issues found and resolved

- The original role model was a strong foundation: subjects occupy the
  grammatical starting slot, while object forms follow verbs and prepositions.
  The revision preserves that model and makes every closed decision identify
  the exact subject, object, preposition, speaker, listener, group, or thing.
- The former lesson asked learners to infer `him` or `her` from names. Every
  person-specific item now states the person’s subject pronoun first. The theory
  explicitly says not to guess a pronoun from a name or appearance.
- `They / them` previously appeared only as plural. Singular `them` now receives
  an explicit A1 use, as in `A customer left a message. Please call them back`,
  while plural people and things remain clearly distinguished by context.
- The identical spelling of object-pronoun `her` and possessive-adjective `her`
  is now resolved through role and position: `The manager called her` contrasts
  with `She called her manager`.
- A compact same-person boundary prevents the lesson from licensing `Maya
  called her` when Maya called herself. Reflexive forms are previewed only far
  enough to distinguish the meaning; they are not turned into a second lesson.
- Pronoun reference now distinguishes the same known thing with `it / them`
  from another example with `one / ones`. It also asks learners to repeat a
  name or noun whenever one pronoun could have two plausible references.
- Two-object placement now gives a neutral U.S. default: `Send her the link`
  and `Send the link to her` work with a full noun, while a thing pronoun is
  normally ordered `Send it to her`, not the awkward learner pattern `Send her
  it`.
- All 49 scored decisions provide a contextual first hint, an exact repair, and
  concept-specific correct feedback. Typed inputs have descriptive accessible
  labels, and coordinated-object repairs distinguish `Ana and I` as subject
  from `Ana and me` as object.
- Spoken guidance covers neutral linking, contrastive focus, light `h` in
  unstressed `him / her`, and reduced `them` for recognition without teaching
  reduced spellings. A six-item tutor-read check tests role, singular reference,
  same-versus-another thing, and pronoun order before its script opens.
- Production no longer requires disclosure of real family, contacts, messages,
  phone use, workplace, relationships, schedule, or location. A fictional
  community-event update requires role changes, singular and plural reference,
  natural placement, connected speech, and reference repair.
- The linked next-day route retrieves four roles and references and repairs a
  coordinated object before the learner rereads the lesson.

## Imperatives: issues found and resolved

- The original lesson already connected imperatives to directions,
  instructions, warnings, offers, encouragement, requests, and suggestions.
  That social-action foundation remains, while every closed choice now states
  the route, rule, danger, relationship, interface, or shared plan that makes
  one form and delivery defensible.
- “The hidden subject is always you” was too absolute. The lesson now says an
  ordinary imperative addresses the listener or listeners, understood as
  `you`, and normally leaves that addressee unspoken. Explicit `you` remains
  possible for emphasis or contrast, and names such as `Maya` can identify the
  addressee outside the core instruction.
- The base-verb system remains compact: positive imperatives use the base form,
  while negatives use `don’t + base verb`, including `Don’t be late`. Full `Do
  not` is now available for signs, formal rules, and firm warnings; positive
  `do` is limited to warmth or insistence rather than taught as the default.
- `Please` was previously said to “make it polite.” The revision treats it as a
  softener, not a guarantee. Relationship, choice, urgency, and delivery still
  matter, and `Can you...?` is preferred when a stranger, coworker, or manager
  can reasonably accept or refuse.
- `Let’s` now has an explicit apostrophe and meaning boundary from present-
  simple `lets`. `Let’s not` forms a negative shared suggestion, while `Let me`
  can offer, announce, or request permission for the speaker’s action depending
  on context.
- Procedural language still uses `first, next, then, after that, finally`, with
  one main action per step. The final task requires ordered content rather than
  a disconnected list of imperative forms.
- The former error task treated `You turn left` as universally impossible. It
  now explicitly requests a neutral, unmarked direction with no listener
  contrast, making `you` the one extra word while preserving its legitimate
  emphatic use elsewhere.
- All 49 scored decisions provide a contextual first hint, exact repair, and
  concept-specific correct feedback. Typed inputs have descriptive accessible
  labels and no longer request a learner’s real name.
- Spoken guidance now covers action and noun focus, helpful versus urgent
  intonation, compact U.S. `don’t`, a light or unreleased final `t`, and audible
  final `s` in `let’s`. A six-item tutor-read check distinguishes neutral
  instruction, offer, request, warning, and positive or negative shared plans.
- Production no longer requests a learner’s real home route, cooking, devices,
  contacts, schedule, workplace, health, or travel. A fictional conference
  orientation requires ordered instructions, safety language, offer, request,
  shared plans, and three distinct spoken deliveries.
- The linked next-day route retrieves four social-action openings and repairs
  the apostrophe in `let’s` before the learner rereads the lesson.
