Grammar teaching often swings between two unsatisfying extremes. In one, the teacher explains for a long time while the learner listens politely. In the other, the lesson avoids explanation so completely that the learner must somehow absorb a pattern through exposure alone.

The Thinking Method takes a more practical route: give the learner a small, carefully chosen sample; help them notice what changes; turn that discovery into a clear mental pattern; and then build enough varied use for the pattern to become available in communication.

The learner is not left to guess. The learner is invited to think.

## Start with a contrast worth noticing

Discovery works when the example is small enough to inspect. A page full of sentences creates noise. Two or three contrasting lines can create a useful question.

Consider:

> I work from home on Fridays.
> She works from home on Fridays.
> Does she work from home on Fridays?

Before naming a rule, ask:

- What changes with *she*?
- Where does the *-s* go in the question?
- Which word now carries the present-tense signal?

The learner can see that *works* becomes *does … work*. That observation is more memorable than receiving the rule as an isolated fact because the mind has already encountered the problem the rule solves.

This is guided discovery, not a trick. If the learner cannot see the pattern, the tutor narrows the question, highlights the relevant words, or explains directly. Thinking time should clarify language, not create anxiety.

## Turn the observation into a portable idea

After noticing comes a concise explanation. The purpose of theory is to give the learner a mental tool that can travel to a new sentence.

For the example above, the portable idea might be:

> In a present-simple question with *he, she,* or *it*, *does* carries the tense. The main verb returns to its base form.

That statement connects form and function. It also predicts new language: *Does he live here? Does it cost much?* The learner does not need a long history of English auxiliaries in the middle of an A1 lesson. They need the root pattern, the reason the visible form changes, and enough examples to test the idea.

A useful explanation answers three questions:

1. **Form:** What changes in the sentence?
2. **Meaning:** What idea does the pattern express?
3. **Use:** In which real situations would someone choose it?

If theory cannot help the learner make a decision, it is probably too detached from the task.

## Build before demanding fluency

Understanding a pattern is not the same as retrieving it quickly. The learner needs a bridge between “I see why” and “I can say it.” Controlled building provides that bridge.

The first practice should isolate one decision at a time. For example:

- choose *do* or *does*;
- restore the base verb after *does*;
- order five tiles into one question;
- change *She works here* into a question;
- correct *Does she works here?*

These are constrained tasks, and that is a strength. Because the expected answer is clear, feedback can be clear. The learner can focus on the target pattern instead of simultaneously solving unfamiliar vocabulary, spelling, interpretation, and grammar.

## Drill for flexibility, not mechanical display

Repetition matters. A learner cannot reason through every auxiliary from the beginning during a real conversation. Useful language must become easier to retrieve.

But a drill should not repeat one sentence until attention disappears. It should keep the pattern stable while changing a meaningful detail:

1. Does **she work** on Fridays?
2. Does **your brother work** on Fridays?
3. Where does **your brother work**?
4. Does he **study** there too?
5. What time does he **start**?

The structure receives repeated attention, but the learner must remain awake to subject, verb, question word, and meaning. This is deliberate variation.

Feedback also matters. A correct answer should confirm the exact pattern. An incorrect answer should help the learner compare forms and try again. “Wrong” alone teaches very little. “After *does*, use the base verb: *work*, not *works*” gives the next attempt a direction.

## Move from tiles to a real person

Controlled practice is preparation, not the destination. The pattern needs to leave the exercise and enter communication.

After building routine questions, the tutor can remove the written model and ask the learner to interview a real person—or interview the tutor—about work, study, family, or weekly habits. The learner chooses what to ask, listens to an answer, and follows up.

A simple production sequence might be:

- prepare three questions;
- ask them without reading full sentences;
- listen and write one key detail from each answer;
- report what the other person does;
- ask one spontaneous follow-up.

Now the grammar has a communicative job. The learner must retrieve the form, understand a response, and adapt. Small errors are expected. The important evidence is whether the new pattern supports a successful exchange.

Every lesson should reach this stage. Explanation without production can feel satisfying while leaving ability unchanged.

## Use transfer knowledge carefully

Adult learners already have a complete language system. Their first language offers concepts, expectations, and shortcuts. It can accelerate English when the patterns align and create predictable errors when they do not.

A Spanish or Portuguese speaker, for example, may expect a present-tense verb ending to stay on the main verb inside a question. English instead moves tense marking to *does*. A Turkish speaker may expect question structure or article choices to work differently. Briefly naming the contrast can save many blind repetitions.

Transfer work should never reduce a learner to a stereotype. Not every speaker makes every predicted error, and biography, education, dialect, and other languages matter. Treat transfer as a hypothesis to test: “Is your first-language pattern pulling this sentence in another direction?”

## Keep the six-stage lesson contour

The method becomes reliable when each lesson follows a recognizable arc:

### Notice

Meet a tiny sample and identify the important contrast. The tutor asks a question that directs attention without giving away every answer.

### Discover

State the pattern in plain language. Connect form, meaning, and use. Check the idea against one new example.

### Build

Construct accurate examples with support. Use constrained choices so one target remains clear.

### Drill

Repeat the pattern with deliberate variation, immediate feedback, oral transformations, and increasing speed.

### Communicate

Use the language for a realistic personal purpose. Remove enough support to reveal whether the learner can retrieve the pattern.

### Reflect

Name what became clearer, identify what remains unstable, and choose the next real situation where the language should appear.

This contour is not a rigid timetable. A difficult discovery may need more examples. A confident learner may move quickly into communication and then return to one repair drill. The stages describe the learning work that must happen, not a script the tutor must obey minute by minute.

## Know when to explain directly

Not every point needs a discovery sequence. If the pattern is arbitrary, the learner is tired, or the contrast would require too much unfamiliar language, a concise direct explanation may be kinder and faster.

The real question is not “Did the teacher explain?” It is “Did the learner build a usable mental model and test it?” Even after direct explanation, ask the learner to predict, compare, build, and communicate. Thinking is still active.

Likewise, do not turn every learner response into a grammar interrogation. Fluency work needs room. During a role play, note selected errors and protect the communicative flow. Afterward, choose the one or two repairs with the greatest value.

## A planning test for tutors

Before teaching a grammar point, try answering these six questions:

1. What tiny contrast will make the pattern visible?
2. What should the learner notice before I explain?
3. What is the shortest accurate rule that predicts a new example?
4. Which constrained task isolates the hardest decision?
5. How will the drill vary without becoming confusing?
6. What personal task will prove the learner can use it?

If the lesson has answers for all six, it is likely to feel both clear and active.

The Thinking Method does not remove theory. It gives theory a better place: after curiosity begins and before practice proves whether the idea works. The result is grammar that feels less like a collection of commands and more like a system the learner can understand, test, and use.
