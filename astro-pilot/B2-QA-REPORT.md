## Third & mixed conditionals: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson had a sound basic explanation but compressed Third and
  Mixed Conditionals into fourteen scored decisions. The revision now contains
  62 contextual decisions across retrieval, noticing, time mapping, form,
  modal meaning, evidence, standard usage, inversion, listening, reading,
  building, repair, production, and delayed retrieval.
- Counterfactual reasoning now begins with an actual layer and an alternative
  layer. Learners state what happened, identify the unreal condition, and
  locate the proposed consequence before choosing a named pattern.
- The Third Conditional maps an unreal past condition to an unreal past result.
  Mixed-time work separately covers past-to-present state, past-to-current
  activity, continuing-state-to-past result, and past-to-future consequence.
  The two familiar mixed patterns are anchors rather than a false complete
  inventory.
- Positive and negative past conditions, positive and negative modal-perfect
  results, information questions, and yes-or-no questions retain every
  auxiliary and participle. Question order stays in the result question while
  statement order remains inside the `if`-clause.
- Past participles and auxiliary order are explicit through `found, written,
  chosen, left`, `would not have been`, and contextual form decisions. The
  repair sequence targets `if...would have`, base `find` after `might have`,
  and a past result where the stated consequence is happening now.
- `Would have` marks the speaker’s expected consequence inside the imagined
  model, not scientific proof. `Could have` marks an ability or available
  opportunity, while `might have` marks an uncertain result. Each controlled
  item supplies evidence strong enough to justify the intended modal.
- The lesson separates known fact, available opportunity, uncertain
  counterfactual result, unsupported blame, and `should have` evaluation.
  Later knowledge is not treated as proof that one person should have predicted
  the exact outcome earlier.
- Careful standard English uses Past Perfect in the unreal past `if`-clause.
  Informal `if...would have` varieties are acknowledged without making them the
  target for academic or professional analysis.
- `Would` in an `if`-clause remains available for a different meaning, such as
  willingness in a formal request. This prevents a useful pattern from being
  incorrectly banned by a simplified rule.
- Formal conditional inversion is introduced through `Had the team tested...`
  and negative `Had the team not restored...`. It is labeled a selective
  formal resource rather than a compulsory upgrade.
- U.S. spoken guidance distinguishes contracted `had` from `would` through the
  following participle or base verb. It covers `would’ve` /ˈwʊdəv/, `could’ve`
  /ˈkʊdəv/, and `might’ve` /ˈmaɪtəv/, explicitly rejects written `would of`, and
  uses thought groups and contrastive stress to preserve the two timelines.
- The five-item tutor-read check tests `had` versus `would`, weak `have`, modal
  certainty, condition-result chunking, and the present endpoint of a mixed
  conditional before written support is used.
- The fictional Greenline file supplies the May 3 launch, documented device
  coverage, three omitted older models accounting for 18% of April mobile
  visits, 64 affected users, rollback request and approval times, 51 restored
  bookings, 13 resubmissions, and 29 next-morning backlog requests.
- Greenline’s review preserves evidence limits. Older-device testing might have
  revealed the problem; preapproval could have reduced the 28-minute approval
  wait; an offline form and import process probably would have reduced the
  backlog. None of those claims proves one person caused the whole outcome.
- Five reading decisions require exact reconstruction of device coverage,
  affected booking counts, the rollback opportunity, the mixed-time endpoint,
  and the safeguard recommendation.
- Two randomized builders create a calibrated past possibility and a
  past-to-present consequence. Three error repairs give contextual first hints,
  exact fixes, and form-plus-meaning explanations.
- All 62 scored decisions have an actual fact, alternative condition, explicit
  time marker, modal intention, evidence relationship, register condition,
  spoken line, reading record, or exact repair target. Every item has a
  contextual first hint, precise fix, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Private-safe practice never requires a real mistake, disciplinary action,
  missed opportunity, medical decision, financial loss, relationship,
  immigration choice, academic failure, or workplace incident.
- Final production requires the actual timeline, available-at-the-time
  evidence, one Third Conditional, two distinct mixed relationships, three
  modal meanings, a negative, a question, selective inversion, uncertainty,
  a safeguard, and live reformulation when a causal link is challenged.
- Delayed retrieval revisits the actual-alternative distinction, complete Third
  Conditional form, past-to-present mapping, modal calibration, and negative
  inversion.

## Wish / if only: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson gave an accurate compact overview but compressed unreal
  present, past regret, desired change, ability, reading, and production into
  18 scored checks. The revision contains 72 contextual decisions across
  retrieval, noticing, time and attitude, complete form, meaning boundaries,
  listening, reading, building, repair, final production, and delayed
  retrieval.
- The lesson begins with the actual fact and preferred alternative. Learners
  locate the real situation as present, completed past, or open future before
  choosing a form. This prevents the shortcut that a past-looking form always
  means past time.
- Four core relationships remain distinct: Past Simple for a different present
  state, `could + base` for unavailable present ability, Past Perfect for a
  different completed past, and `would + base` for a desired change in
  behavior or a process.
- `If only` is presented as a common emotional intensifier rather than a new
  tense system. Longing, frustration, and regret remain context-dependent, so
  the lesson does not impose one emotion or one intonation on every use.
- Counterfactual `were` is the reliable careful target with every subject.
  Natural informal U.S. `was` with `I, he, she,` and `it` is acknowledged so
  the lesson does not create a false absolute error rule.
- Positive and negative present alternatives, present `be`, unavailable
  ability, positive and negative past alternatives, and positive and negative
  desired changes keep the required subject, auxiliary, base verb, or
  participle.
- Questions invert the matrix clause while the embedded preferred reality
  keeps statement order. `What do you wish you had known?` is contrasted with
  the incorrect `had you known` sequence after `wish`.
- `Wish` and `hope` are separated through outcome status. `Hope` fits a future
  result that remains open; Past Perfect `wish` fits a completed reality the
  speaker cannot now change.
- Formal `wish to + base` as formal `want to`, and conventional `wish + person
  + noun`, remain distinct from counterfactual clauses.
- `Would` is limited to a plausible desired change or marked willingness and
  recurring-behavior contexts. Stable current location, knowledge, and
  characteristics use Past Simple instead of a mechanical `would` pattern.
- `I wish I had attended` directly regrets nonattendance. `I wish I could have
  attended` highlights an unavailable past opportunity, with an obstacle added
  when the distinction matters.
- U.S. spoken guidance covers linked `wish I`, contracted `had` before a
  participle, contracted `would` before a base verb, reduced `could have`,
  negative Past Perfect, and contrastive stress on the preferred reality.
- The five-item tutor-read sequence tests present location, contracted `had`,
  contracted `would`, reduced past ability, and a stressed regretted action
  before written support is used.
- The fictional Pine Street file establishes the September 2 registration
  launch, September 15 open day, capacity 60, 74 accepted registrations, the
  reserved second room, four tested device environments, three omitted older
  phone models, 43 attempts on those models, and 18 incomplete attempts.
- Pine Street's reminder record fixes the messages at 8:00 and 8:05, vendor
  confirmation at 9:10, and testing still in progress at 11:30 before an open
  noon forecast. This supports desired-change `would` and future `hope` in the
  same case without confusing their time relationships.
- The case explicitly limits the evidence. Older-phone testing is a reasonable
  safeguard, but the record does not prove compatibility caused every
  incomplete attempt, that one person could have predicted every problem, or
  that one person caused the result.
- Eight reading decisions require exact reconstruction of dates, the
  14-registration capacity mismatch, the second-room restriction, omitted
  phone testing, model-specific attempt counts, duplicate timing, the open
  forecast, and the evidence limit.
- Three randomized builders create a present counterfactual, a Past Perfect
  regret, and a desired process change. Four repairs target state `would`, a
  missing Past Perfect auxiliary, stable-state `would be`, and incorrect
  embedded question order.
- All 72 scored decisions have a real fact, preferred alternative, time,
  attitude, ability limit, changeable behavior, register, spoken cue, case-file
  detail, evidence boundary, or exact repair target. Each has a contextual
  first hint, precise fix, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Private-safe practice never requires a real relationship, medical decision,
  financial loss, immigration matter, disciplinary event, academic failure,
  family conflict, legal issue, or painful regret.
- Final production requires a present state, unavailable ability, two past
  regrets, desired change, open hope, a negative, an embedded question,
  contracted `’d`, an evidence limit, a next step, and live reformulation when
  the listener challenges the time or attitude.
- Delayed retrieval revisits unreal present state, unavailable ability, Past
  Perfect regret, desired change, and the wish-hope boundary.

## Cleft sentences for emphasis: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson presented the core patterns accurately but compressed the
  topic into 13 scored checks. The revision contains 74 contextual decisions
  across retrieval, noticing, focus selection, form, boundary distinctions,
  listening, reading, building, repair, final checks, and delayed retrieval.
- Every cleft now begins with a recoverable neutral proposition. Learners
  identify the person, thing, time, place, reason, action, need, concern, or
  result selected as focus before choosing an it-cleft, what-cleft, reversed
  pseudo-cleft, reason pattern, or all-cleft.
- Information structure is tied to discourse purpose. The lesson distinguishes
  first reporting from correction, treats the nonfocused proposition as
  background, and warns that a grammatical cleft can introduce an unsupported
  presupposition or make a neutral report sound theatrical or defensive.
- Subject and object gaps are explicit. Controlled work prevents repeated
  subjects and objects, mismatched person links, lost tense, and cleft frames
  that do not preserve the original participant relationship.
- Pronoun case and agreement are presented as real variation rather than false
  absolutes. Careful `It was I who called` and natural conversational U.S.
  English `It was me who called` are both acknowledged. Singular agreement
  after a what-clause is a useful default, while plural agreement is recognized
  where the speaker construes a plural complement.
- `The reason is because` is described as widespread rather than impossible.
  The lesson teaches `The reason ... was that ...` as a concise edited target.
  It likewise recognizes both `All I did was call` and `All I did was to call`,
  with the bare form common in U.S. English.
- It-clefts are separated from referential `it` plus a relative clause,
  defining relatives, what-clefts, embedded questions, and neutral declaratives.
  Standard `that` remains the portable target for time and place without
  pretending that all natural variants are ungrammatical.
- Spoken work covers reduced `it was` /ɪtwəz/, weak `that` /ðət/, linked
  `what we` /ˈwʌtwi/, and moving nuclear stress. Learners hear that the intended
  contrast, not the written pattern alone, determines the prominent word.
- The fictional Lakeside record separates a 27% reduction at three
  intersections, a six-month independent study, a petition, a budget memo, a
  6-2 vote, and a limited second trial. Learners must keep documented cause,
  meeting time, evidence source, and policy scope distinct.
- Evidence is a hard boundary throughout. A cleft selects information focus;
  it cannot prove political pressure, guarantee citywide effects, or turn a
  six-month extension into permanent adoption.
- The final production requires neutral propositions and sources before seven
  focus patterns, subject and object gaps, register-sensitive pronouns, varied
  tense, nuclear stress, an explicit presupposition, and a neutral alternative.
  Private-safe fictional records avoid requiring real disputes or accusations.
- Generated pedagogy remains marked for tutor review. No learner pilot has been
  claimed, and the lesson cannot move to reviewed status until three real
  sessions are documented and substantive findings are resolved.

## Advanced discourse markers: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson had a strong functional overview but compressed the topic
  into 13 scored decisions. The revision contains 74 contextual decisions
  across retrieval, noticing, relation mapping, form, position, punctuation,
  function boundaries, listening, reading, building, repair, production, and
  delayed retrieval.
- Learners now name the relationship before selecting vocabulary. Addition,
  comparison, concession, counterexpectation, direct correction, consequence,
  inference, example, specification, reformulation, stance, scope, and weighed
  conclusion are kept distinct through complete source-based contexts.
- Concession is taught as a two-move relationship. A learner first grants a
  relevant limitation and then decides whether a supported conclusion survives
  it. The B2 assessment pair `Admittedly ... Nevertheless ...` is practiced
  without treating either marker as evidence.
- `On the contrary` is restricted to direct rejection of a proposition. It is
  contrasted with `whereas`, `by contrast`, and `on the other hand`, which can
  compare distinct cases without declaring the first claim false.
- Form work now covers sentence connectors, semicolon-linked connectors,
  medial parentheticals, subordinators, prepositional concession, concessive
  `however + adjective`, `thereby + -ing`, and parallel `not only ... but also`.
  Controlled items preserve complete clause roles and parallel structure.
- Contrastive `However, ...`, medial `..., however, ...`, and concessive
  `However short ...` are separated by syntax, punctuation, scope, and rhythm.
  The lesson no longer implies that one visible comma pattern fits every use.
- Result and inference markers are bounded by evidence. `Therefore` and
  `consequently` display a proposed bridge but do not prove causation. `Thereby`
  names a result produced through an action and is followed by an `-ing` form.
- Reformulation, precision, specification, and example are treated as different
  promises. `In other words` preserves substantially the same message, `more
  precisely` narrows it, `namely` identifies exact members, and `for example`
  supplies a nonexhaustive illustration.
- Register work rejects the idea that longer markers are automatically more
  advanced. Learners move between formal reinforcement and natural spoken
  `but`, `still`, `so`, `actually`, `I mean`, and `at the same time`, and remove
  unnecessary markers when order and reference already make the relation clear.
- U.S. speech guidance corrects the previous inaccurate initial-stress model
  for `nevertheless`. It now gives /ˌnevərðəˈles/ with primary stress on the
  final syllable, plus framed `admittedly`, weak `that` before stressed `said`,
  light medial `however`, and a final nucleus after `on balance`.
- The fictional Westbridge record fixes trial dates, operating hours, capacity,
  attendance, survey design, unmeasured outcomes, funding, and proposal scope.
  Learners must distinguish observed visits from unique users, voluntary survey
  results from population estimates, and a measured extension from a permanent
  citywide program.
- Final production requires ten distinct logical moves, eight form and position
  patterns, formal and conversational versions, marker removal, listener
  challenge, and immediate calibration when a source does not support an arrow.
  Private-safe fictional work avoids requiring personal political or workplace
  disclosure.
- Generated pedagogy remains marked for tutor review. No learner pilot has been
  claimed, and review status cannot advance until three real sessions are
  documented and substantive findings are resolved.

## Abstract & academic topics: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson provided a useful reasoning vocabulary bank but compressed
  the topic into 13 scored decisions. The revision contains 74 contextual
  decisions across retrieval, noticing, reasoning functions, word families,
  collocations, countability, claim strength, listening, reading, building,
  repair, production, and delayed retrieval.
- Academic vocabulary is now anchored to a visible reasoning job. Observation,
  finding, data, variable, association, causal effect, mechanism, hypothesis,
  assumption, interpretation, limitation, implication, and recommendation are
  separated through one coherent fictional study.
- Association and causation are no longer taught as a mechanical ban. The
  lesson explains why an uncontrolled association does not establish cause and
  why random allocation can support a bounded causal inference when
  implementation and other design conditions are credible.
- A proposed mechanism is kept separate from an observed effect. Reduced
  forgotten bookings is labeled as an untested explanation because Cedar Grove
  measured attendance, not memory.
- Word-family work covers `analyze, assume, conclude, interpret, vary`, and
  `significant` across verbs, nouns, and adjectives, with accurate stress
  movement and contextual word-class decisions.
- `Evidence` and `research` are taught as normally uncountable in ordinary
  academic English, with pieces, lines, sources, studies, and articles as
  countable units. The lesson acknowledges genuine `data` variation: plural in
  much formal scientific style and singular mass usage in general U.S. English.
- Collocation work includes conducting studies and research, collecting and
  analyzing data, evaluating evidence, drawing conclusions from sources,
  questioning assumptions, identifying limitations, contributing factors,
  accounting for variation, implications for, and both result directions.
- Reporting verbs are tied to design and evidence rather than placed on a
  permanent caution ladder. Direct records, association, cautious
  interpretation, bounded causal effects, strong narrowly documented facts,
  and unsupported proof claims each receive a distinct context.
- Observed difference, statistical significance, and practical importance are
  separated. The lesson does not label the nine-percentage-point result
  statistically significant because no test or confidence interval is given,
  and it makes cost, capacity, effect size, and goals part of practical value.
- Scope is treated as part of the proposition. Population, branches, eight-week
  period, reminder condition, and attendance outcome remain visible so the
  learner cannot silently generalize to every adult or replace attendance with
  learning or employment.
- Dense nominalization is presented as a choice, not a marker of intelligence.
  Learners preserve useful concepts while restoring the source, agent, and
  action when stacked nouns make the meaning opaque.
- U.S. speech work covers stress shifts from `ANalyze` to `aNALysis`, correct
  first-syllable stress in `INdicate`, linking in `results indicate` and `factor
  in`, reduced `for`, final information stress, and contrastive scope chunks.
- The fictional Cedar Grove file provides dates, 600 opt-in adults, random
  300-person groups, 18 workshops, exact attendance counts and percentages,
  missing uncertainty and delivery analysis, a $1,260 service cost, unmeasured
  outcomes, bounded causal scope, and a proportionate next evaluation.
- Final production requires a complete evidence-to-implication briefing, eight
  collocations, four word families, countability, one consistent `data` style,
  both meanings of significance, dense and plain versions, design challenge,
  and immediate recalibration. Private-safe practice avoids real personal data.
- Generated pedagogy remains marked for tutor review. No learner pilot has been
  claimed, and review status cannot advance until three real sessions are
  documented and substantive findings are resolved.

## Non-defining relative clauses: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson supplied a useful compact contrast but added only 13 scored
  decisions beyond the B1 prerequisite. The revision contains 74 contextual

  preposition and quantity patterns, whole-clause attachment, punctuation,
  listening, reading, building, repair, production, and delayed retrieval.
- The 58-decision B1 defining-clause lesson remains the foundation for noun
  reference, subject and object gaps, omission, agreement, `who`, `which`,
  `that`, `whose`, `where`, prepositions, and continuous spoken integration.
  The B2 lesson retrieves those decisions and advances to parenthetical meaning
  rather than reteaching identification as new.
- Defining and non-defining are presented as discourse choices, not noun
  classes. `My brother who lives in Seoul` can identify one of several
  brothers, while commas are appropriate when the conversation has already
  established one brother. `The Jordan Lee who works for Northline` shows that
  even a proper name can need a defining clue in a namesake context.
- A paired consultant record makes punctuation scope measurable. Two of six
  consultants speak Spanish in the defining version; all three members of an
  already selected group speak Spanish in the non-defining version.

  the writer, or automatically true. Removing the insertion preserves the
  referent but removes a separate assertion.
- Person subject `who`, person object `who/whom`, thing or proposition `which`,
  `whose + noun`, place `where`, and time `when` are tied to their roles inside
  complete clauses. `Whose` extends naturally to organizations and things.

  `he`, `she`, `it`, `there`, or another object.
