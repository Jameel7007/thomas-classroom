## Past simple: issues found and resolved

- The previous lesson treated a time expression beside the verb as the main
  trigger for past simple. The revision starts from a speaker’s finished-past
  viewpoint and shows how one time expression can establish the frame for a
  connected report.
- Form, meaning, and use now cover a single finished event, a completed event
  sequence, a past state, and repetition inside a finished period. The page
  explicitly avoids claiming that every time period containing a past event
  must itself be over.
- The old consonant-doubling rule said that one vowel plus one consonant was
  sufficient. The corrected reference requires a short stressed vowel before
  the final consonant, distinguishes `planned / stopped` from `opened`, and
  blocks doubling final `w`, `x`, and `y` in `snowed, fixed, played`.
- Consonant-plus-`y`, vowel-plus-`y`, final `e`, and ordinary `-ed` spelling are
  separated. Frequent irregular forms remain high-frequency and are stored as
  spoken base-past pairs rather than as a claim that irregular grammar is
  random.
- Questions now include yes-or-no, information, and short-answer forms. The
  one-past-marker explanation connects positives to `did / didn't + base verb`
  and contrasts that system with independent past `was / were` questions.
- The distinction between `Who called Rina?` and `Who did Rina call?` exposes
  subject and object question logic without requiring a memorized exception.
- `-ed` pronunciation is now selected by the final sound of the base verb, not
  by spelling. The lesson distinguishes /t/, /d/, and /ɪd/, syllable count,
  voicing, linked `did you`, and information stress in event sequences.
- A six-item tutor-read check distinguishes all three regular endings, positive
  and negative meaning, a `did` question, and a past state before its transcript
  opens.
- All 49 scored decisions include a finished time, established discourse frame,
  stated question purpose, event-log fact, or exact sound condition. Every item
  has a contextual first hint, exact repair, and concept-specific correct
  feedback.
- The former production section required true details about work, travel,
  routines, family, home, and personal history. Fictional community-event cards
  now provide equivalent evidence while real disclosure remains optional.
- The final 45-to-60-second report requires a finished time, ordered actions,
  regular and irregular forms, a negative, a past state, and a useful follow-up
  question. Next-day retrieval revisits spelling, the base verb after `didn't`,
  past `be`, and the single-marker question pattern.

## Feelings & personality: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson organized vocabulary around a fixed “feeling or
  personality” binary. The revision separates four description jobs: current or
  past state, reaction and stimulus, general tendency, and observable behavior.
- `Be + adjective` and `feel + adjective` are both retained, while their
  viewpoints are clarified. `Be` presents a state and `feel` foregrounds the
  experiencer’s internal state. The separate `feel like + noun / -ing` pattern
  is introduced without treating it as interchangeable.
- The former rule that `-ed` describes a person and `-ing` describes a thing was
  removed. `-ed` normally presents the experiencer’s reaction and `-ing` the
  stimulus or effect. A person can be `interesting` or `encouraging`, and a
  group or organization can be `interested`.
- Controlled practice now requires the learner to identify the meaning role
  from a complete situation before choosing `interested / interesting`,
  `confused / confusing`, or `surprised / surprising`.
- Adjective complements are stored as natural chunks: `interested in`, `excited
  about`, `worried / nervous about`, `surprised by / at`, and common `angry`
  patterns. They are presented as strong tendencies rather than false bans on
  every alternative.
- Degree language now distinguishes `a little / a bit`, `really / very`, and
  `extremely / completely`. Exercises preserve the degree supported by the
  speaker instead of turning a small worry into an extreme emotion.
- Personality adjectives are no longer described as permanently or universally
  true. `Usually, often, can be`, and a situation phrase limit a generalization
  to what repeated evidence supports.
- `Friendly, patient, quiet, funny, hardworking`, and `direct` are tied to
  possible observable evidence and explicit boundaries. One smile, one quiet
  meeting, or one late task does not prove a global identity.
- `Quiet` is separated from `shy, unfriendly`, and `unhappy`. Amount of speech
  can be observed, but a private feeling or motivation requires evidence or the
  person’s own report.
- `Fun` and `funny` are separated: `fun` is enjoyable, while `funny` commonly
  means humorous and can also mean strange.
- The page models behavior before label for useful feedback. `Maya explained
  the process twice and waited for questions` provides better evidence than the
  unsupported global description `Maya is nice`.
- Current condition, character, preference, and appearance now use distinct
  questions: `How is she feeling?`, `What’s she like?`, `What does she like?`,
  and `What does she look like?` A context such as `to work with` can narrow a
  broad character question.
- The learner is told not to diagnose a feeling from appearance alone. `You
  look worried` is framed as an impression, while a direct feeling question
  allows the person to report their own state.
- Spoken guidance covers /d/, /t/, and /ɪd/ in participial adjectives, common
  U.S. syllable reduction in `interested`, `/ɪŋ/` without a hard final `g`,
  connected `/haʊɚjə ˈfilɪŋ/`, question stress, and contrastive repair of
  `interested / interesting`.
- A six-item tutor-read check distinguishes current feeling and degree,
  stimulus effect, repeated evidence, a context-limited tendency, preference
  question, and experiencer-role correction before its transcript opens.
- All 49 scored decisions include a time boundary, meaning role, complement,
  degree cue, repeated behavior, context limit, question purpose, reading fact,
  listening fact, or exact repair context. Every item has a contextual first
  hint, exact fix, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Real feelings, mental health, personality, relationships, family, workplace,
  coworkers, home, schedule, and personal history remain optional. Fictional
  Harbor team evidence supplies equivalent production.
- The final 45-to-60-second introduction requires a feeling with cause, an
  experiencer-stimulus relationship, two evidence-based tendencies, a context
  limit, an observation repair, and a follow-up question. Next-day retrieval
  revisits `interested in`, behavior versus label, and `bored / boring`.

## Technology & devices: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson centered on gadget names and isolated commands. The
  revision starts from the real communication problem: another person needs a
  device or service, attempted action, observable symptom, time, scope, and
  exact message before offering a useful next step.
- Technology vocabulary is organized into five layers: hardware, app or
  software, connection or network, account or access, and file or location.
  This replaces the vague report `My computer is broken` with a precise target.
- Observable evidence is separated from diagnosis. Learners report that a page
  will not load or a progress bar stays at zero instead of declaring that a
  router, battery, or internet connection is broken without evidence.
- `Download / upload`, `download / install`, `save / back up`, `restart /
  reset`, `connect / pair`, and `mute / turn off` now have distinct actions and
  results. The page explicitly warns that a reset may alter settings or erase
  information and is not a synonym for restart.
- Separable phrasal verbs retain both noun positions, while pronouns must occur
  inside the chunk: `turn on the laptop / turn the laptop on / turn it on`.
  Fixed-preposition patterns such as `log in to`, `connect to`, and `search
  for` are kept separate from that rule.
- The two natural `send` patterns are modeled as `send me the link` and `send
  the link to me`; the lesson does not overgeneralize one object-placement rule
  to all technology verb combinations.
- Symptom language now covers failure to turn on, a frozen screen, a page that
  will not load, missing sound, a battery that is not charging, and inability
  to log in. Each controlled item includes a visible symptom plus a device,
  time, comparison, or exact message.
- `Will not / won’t` is explained as failure or refusal to perform an action in
  a problem report, rather than as a future prediction.
- Support responses use one authorized, low-risk step followed by an evidence
  check. They do not promise a diagnosis, erase data, install unknown software,
  or make security changes outside the fictional owner’s authorization.
- Account safety now keeps passwords, PINs, and verification codes private,
  rejects unexpected urgent links or attachments, and redirects the learner to
  a known official support channel. The learner-facing boundaries link to
  current FTC and CISA guidance.
- Battery safety now tells the learner to stop using and charging a device when
  the battery becomes unusually hot, changes shape or color, leaks, smells
  strange, or makes odd noises. It prohibits attempted repair and links to U.S.
  Fire Administration guidance.
- Spoken guidance covers common U.S. forms for `Wi-Fi, device, password`, and
  `error`; keeps `won’t` distinct from `want`; places stress on the failed
  action; and links `turn it on, plug it in, can you`, and `could you` naturally.
- A six-item tutor-read check recovers the device, attempted action, observable
  symptom, scope comparison, exact message, and safe account response before
  the transcript opens.
- The reading ticket requires evidence from one complete support report,
  including what works, what was attempted, the exact message, and the safe
  handling of an unexpected support link.
- All 49 scored decisions include a device, destination, action result, object
  structure, symptom, time, comparison, message, authorization boundary,
  reading fact, listening fact, or exact repair context. Every item has a
  contextual first hint, exact fix, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Real devices, employers, schools, networks, usernames, credentials, files,
  messages, contacts, locations, and account histories remain optional. A
  fictional Harbor Learning support card supplies equivalent production.
- The final 45-to-60-second report requires the affected layer, attempted
  action, observable symptom, start time, two scope checks, exact message, and
  safe next-step request. Next-day retrieval revisits pronoun placement,
  upload direction, and symptom-plus-scope reporting.

## Education & study: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson was organized around school objects and isolated
  collocations. The revision begins with the adult communication task: report a
  learning goal, path, routine, evidence, current gap, and next step clearly
  enough that another person can understand the support needed.
- The claim that every education noun has one fixed partner verb was removed.
  The revision teaches whole chunks while preserving meaningful alternatives
  and stages such as `sign up for / register for / enroll in`, `take / attend`,
  and `complete / pass`.
- `Subject, course, class, lesson, program`, and `qualification` now have
  separate jobs. `Class` includes a scheduled meeting and its broader everyday
  U.S. uses, while `lesson` identifies a teaching unit or focus.
- The former universal sequence from elementary school through university was
  removed. The lesson explicitly states that education systems and official
  labels vary and that a certificate, diploma, and degree are not automatic
  synonyms.
- Registering for a course now states entry, attending states presence, taking
  presents participation, completing states finished requirements, and passing
  states that the required standard was met. None is used as automatic evidence
  for another stage.
- Taking an exam is separated from passing or failing it. Controlled items keep
  the result unknown when it has not yet been reported.
- `Homework` is now explicitly noncount in its ordinary meaning. Separate tasks
  are counted with `homework assignments`, while `assignment` remains a
  countable noun.
- `Score, grade`, and `feedback` are separated. A score often provides points
  or a percentage, a grade depends on the stated system, and feedback provides
  comments about strengths and improvement.
- `Study` presents work with a subject, `learn` presents developing knowledge or
  ability, `teach` presents helping another person learn, `practice` presents
  repeated performance, and `review` presents returning to material.
- The page avoids two false absolutes: study does not guarantee learning, and
  learn is not limited to a completed result. The two natural `teach` object
  patterns are preserved.
- Effort and progress are no longer treated as synonyms. Useful evidence can
  compare a before-and-now sample, a stated score, prompts needed, an independent
  can-do, or a specific feedback point.
- Broad requests such as `I don’t understand` are rebuilt as precise classroom
  repair: state what is understood and ask about a term, example, deadline,
  submission place, or required response mode.
- Spoken guidance covers common U.S. forms for `course, learn, study, education,
  assignment`, and noun `progress`; final sounds in `studied, practiced`, and
  `completed`; linked `Could you` and `Where should I`; information stress; and
  learning-update chunking.
- A six-item tutor-read check recovers the learning goal, scheduled class, study
  action, before-and-now evidence, specific feedback, and precise clarification
  before its transcript opens.
- The reading note requires evidence from one connected learning update rather
  than isolated vocabulary recognition. It connects the stated goal, repeated
  practice, reduced prompts, tutor feedback, and next revision.
- All 49 scored decisions include a defined education-system role, registration
  or result boundary, countability condition, learner-teacher direction, study
  purpose, comparison, score, feedback point, reading fact, listening fact, or
  exact repair context. Every item has a contextual first hint, exact fix, and
  concept-specific correct feedback.
- Real schools, employers, programs, teachers, transcripts, grades,
  qualifications, funding, disabilities, immigration details, schedules, and
  learning histories remain optional. Fictional Harbor evidence supplies an
  equivalent production path.
- The final 45-to-60-second update requires the goal, course or subject,
  scheduled class, two study actions, before-and-now evidence, feedback,
  current gap, next step, and one precise question. Next-day retrieval revisits
  homework countability, take-pass meaning, and subject-course-class roles.

## Phrasal verbs: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson defined every verb plus small word as one new phrasal verb.
  The revision uses a practical learner label while acknowledging that grammar
  books may classify fixed verb-preposition chunks differently.
- Learners now store four facts for every target: the whole chunk, its meaning
  in one context, its object pattern, and a complete example. Register is added
  when a neutral and more official alternative differ.
- Particles are no longer presented as making a completely new or predictable
  meaning in every combination. The page distinguishes physical direction,
  result, and meanings that cannot be safely guessed from the individual parts.
- The main verb now carries tense and agreement while the particle remains
  stable. Past forms include regular and irregular heads, and `did / didn’t`
  returns the main verb to its base form.
- The page separates three practical object patterns instead of applying one
  pronoun rule to every chunk: no object in the target meaning, a separable
  object, and a fixed chunk before its object.
- The no-object group is explicitly limited to the meanings shown. Time, place,
  and manner phrases after `got up, went out, came back, sat down, showed up`,
  and `grew up` are not mislabeled as movable direct objects.
- Separable combinations retain both short noun positions, while personal
  pronouns must occur between the main verb and particle. Controlled practice
  covers `turn on, pick up, write down, fill out, throw away`, and `call back`.
- Fixed combinations preserve the order in `look for, wait for, listen to,
  look after, get on`, and `get off`. Learners use `look for them`, not the
  overgeneralized `look them for`.
- Polysemy is now explicit. `Pick up` can lift or collect, `take off` can remove
  clothing or describe a plane leaving the ground, and `turn down` can reduce a
  level or reject an offer. Context and object pattern select the meaning.
- The former implication that phrasal verbs are automatically informal was
  removed. Common combinations such as `fill out a form` remain neutral in
  everyday U.S. English, while official wording may use `complete the form`.
- `Put off / postpone` and similar alternatives are not treated as mechanical
  replacements. Learners follow the context, collocation, tone, and exact
  wording of an official instruction.
- Pronunciation no longer assigns automatic particle stress to every chunk.
  The page distinguishes common result stress in separable chunks from lighter
  prepositions in fixed combinations and notes that contrast can move stress.
- Spoken guidance also covers linked pronouns, final consonant contrasts,
  reduced U.S. `Can you /kənjə/` and `Did you /dɪdʒə/`, and corrective stress
  in `Turn it OFF, not DOWN`.
- A six-item tutor-read check requires a collection meaning, separable pronoun,
  fixed pronoun, level reduction, airplane departure, and a register-based
  clarification question before its transcript opens.
- The reading handoff connects arrival, collection, recording, authorized
  search, equipment setup, returned communication, and responsible record
  handling. It does not test disconnected particle recognition.
- All 49 scored decisions include a stated meaning, time frame, object referent,
  pattern condition, contextual contrast, register cue, reading fact, listening
  fact, or exact repair context. Every item has a contextual first hint, exact
  fix, and concept-specific correct feedback.
- Real homes, workplaces, routes, travel times, phone numbers, addresses, form
  data, passwords, health details, habits, family information, and schedules
  remain optional. A fictional Harbor workshop supplies equivalent production.
- The final 45-to-60-second handoff requires a no-object chunk, two separable
  pronouns, a fixed pronoun, past time, a register alternative, and a meaning
  clarification. Next-day retrieval revisits both object patterns and flight
  `take off`.

## Past-time expressions: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson organized `yesterday`, `last`, and `ago` as three isolated
  families that all answered `When did it happen?` The revision begins with
  reference time and separates five discourse jobs: locate a point, measure a
  duration, order events, show overlap, and recover a shared reference.
- Every relative expression now has an anchor. `Ago` measures backward from the
  speaking moment, `before` measures from another stated or understood event,
  and `earlier / later / then` require a recoverable discourse reference.
- The page no longer implies that `before` is simply an incorrect translation
  of `ago`. It is correct when another event supplies the reference, as in `two
  days before the opening`.
- Calendar location is organized by precision: `in` for a broad year, month, or
  season; `on` for a day or date; and `at` for a clock point. `Yesterday`,
  preceding-calendar `last`, and `ago` work without those prepositions.
- The old absolute rule that `the last week` was wrong was removed. `Last week`
  means the preceding calendar week, while `the last week of the course` means
  its final week and `for the last week` can measure a period reaching now.
- `Last Monday` is identified as potentially ambiguous near week boundaries.
  The lesson recommends an explicit date for bookings, deadlines, and records
  instead of claiming that all speakers calculate the phrase identically.
- Standard U.S. `last night` is retained, while the former claim that English
  “simply never” says another form was narrowed to ordinary U.S. usage.
- Sequence language now establishes a date first and then uses `first, then,
  after that, later`, and `finally`. `Before` and `after` accept a noun phrase

## Present perfect: issues found and resolved

  no time is stated or when time is not over. The revision starts from the
  present connection and separates a current result, experience up to now, a
  continuing state, and an unfinished reporting period.

  years`, `today`, and `so far` now show how a before-now period can reach the
  present, so learners are not taught the false “no time stated” rule.
- A calendar word does not choose the tense automatically. The same word
  `today` can frame an open count at 10:00 or a completed event report after the

## Will for predictions and offers: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson said that `will` was always the grammar of the moment of
  speaking and that its future was never planned in advance. That explanation
  only fits an immediate decision. The revision gives the modal a wider and
  more accurate root: it expresses the speaker’s present stance toward a future
  event through belief, choice, willingness, or commitment.
- Predictions are no longer described as ideas necessarily formed during the
  conversation, and promises are not assumed to be unplanned. The page states
  that predictions may come from an existing belief and promises may renew an
  earlier commitment.

  choice announced at that moment. This preserves the useful A2 contrast with a
  `going to` intention whose decision existed earlier.

  the listener to act. Natural acceptance and refusal language is included.

  third-person `wills` forms are diagnosed and repaired.
- Probability guidance now controls affirmative `will probably`, negative

  presented as a whole, while a brief warning signal can use past continuous

## Comparatives and superlatives: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson reduced the system to short versus long adjectives and
  instructed learners to count syllables. That rule fails for many common
  two-syllable adjectives. The revision teaches stable one-syllable and
  consonant-`y` patterns, useful two-syllable tendencies, common variation, and
  the need to learn a conventional form with the adjective.
- The consonant-doubling rule previously required only one vowel plus one
  consonant. The corrected rule requires a one-syllable consonant-vowel-
  consonant spelling with a short stressed vowel and blocks doubling final
  `w`, `x`, and `y`.
- Comparison now starts with a scale, reference, and set rather than an ending.
  A comparative locates one value relative to another reference, which can be
  one item, a prior situation, or a group. It is not defined as exactly two
  physical objects.
- A superlative identifies an extreme inside a defined set, not a universal
  “number one.” The lesson shows how the truth of a claim changes when the set
  changes and how two items can share an extreme.
- `One of the + superlative + plural noun` now handles membership in a tied top
  group without making a false uniqueness claim.
- The article explanation no longer says every superlative requires `the`.
  Possessives replace it in phrases such as `our largest room`, and a comparison
  set or reference can be omitted when discourse already makes it clear.
- Equality uses base adjectives inside `as...as`. Negative `not as...as` is
  interpreted on the adjective’s scale, so `not as bad` correctly describes a

  measurements, and `twice as...as`. The page separates an invalid double
  comparative such as `more larger` from valid degree modification such as

## Modals: should, must, have to: issues found and resolved

- The former lesson arranged `should`, `have to`, and `must` as a simple
  strength ladder. The revision begins with the action’s status: recommended,
  required, prohibited, or optional. This prevents learners from selecting a
  form by imagined force alone.
- The previous inside-versus-outside authority rule was too absolute. `Must`
  and `have to` overlap and can describe the same obligation. The new contrast
  focuses on framing and register: neutral everyday requirement versus a firm,
  formal, or emphatic presentation.
- Natural U.S. use is explicit. `Have to` is common for ordinary conversational
  requirements, `must` and `must not` fit firm or formal notices, and `can’t`
  or `not allowed to` are common everyday ways to express prohibition.
- The target use of `should` is recommendation, with a clear notice that
  `should` also has later meanings such as expectation. Advice is not presented
  as the universal meaning of the modal.
- Modal form and ordinary-verb form are separated. `Should / must + base verb`
  stays invariant, while `have to` changes to `has to` and uses `do / does` for
  present questions and negatives.

  a modal, and the double-agreement error `Does she has to...?`.
- `Shouldn’t`, `must not / mustn’t`, and `don’t have to` now express three
  different consequences: advice against, prohibition, and lack of necessity.

- The earlier page omitted time beyond the present. The revision adds `had to`,

  `musted` and `will must` forms.
- `Didn’t have to` is accurately limited to lack of past necessity; it does not

  future action when another expression supplies the time.
- Advice and rule questions are distinguished by information purpose. `Should

## Quantifiers: much, many, a lot of: issues found and resolved

- The former page taught questions and negatives as `much / many` territory and
  positives as `a lot of` territory. The revision presents these as useful
  tendencies, not sentence-type laws. `A lot of` also works in questions and
  negatives, while positive `many` is normal in written and formal contexts.
- Plain positive `much` is accurately described as uncommon in everyday U.S.
  conversation, while common positive patterns such as `too much`, `so much`,
  and `as much...as` remain explicit.
- Countability no longer appears to be a permanent physical property of the
  real-world object. The chosen noun meaning packages a quantity as separate

## First conditional: issues found and resolved

- The former page foregrounded a mechanical `if + present, will + base` formula.
  The revision begins with the meaning relationship: one open future condition
  activates a consequence or response.
- A first conditional is no longer labeled automatically likely or unlikely.
  It presents a condition as open and relevant; evidence and context determine
  probability, and the grammar itself does not guarantee the result.
- The false rules “one `will` per sentence” and “`will` always belongs in the
  result” were removed. The A2 rule is scoped precisely: do not use `will` in
  the condition merely as a future marker. Special willingness uses remain
  possible outside this core pattern.
- Future-reference present form now includes third-person agreement, negatives,
  questions about the result, condition-first and result-first order, and the
  comma pattern for an initial condition clause.
- Clause order is not presented as meaningless rearrangement. The two orders
  preserve the core relationship while changing information flow and emphasis.
- Result clauses now express a planned or predicted result with `will / won't`,
  a weaker possibility with `may / might`, permission or available possibility
  with `can`, and an instruction with an imperative.
- `If` leaves the condition open. `When` presents the time event as expected
  without guaranteeing it in the real world. Parallel examples show how the
  same future event can receive either framing.
- `Unless` is taught as “except if” and, in the controlled core pattern, “if
  not.” Exact replacements prevent learners from adding another negative and
  accidentally reversing the intended exception.
- Spoken U.S. guidance covers linked `/ɪf jə/`, full `/wil/` and weak `/wəl/`,
  contrastive stress on `if`, and second-syllable stress in `/ənˈlɛs/`.
- A six-item tutor-read check distinguishes an open condition, expected time,
  exception, planned result, weaker possibility, and imperative result before
  its transcript opens.
- All 49 scored decisions contain an explicit condition status, result purpose,

  requirement, reading fact, listening fact, or exact repair. Every item has a

## Adverbs of manner: issues found and resolved

  how you do it.” The revision begins with the target of the description: a
  noun, a subject quality or state, or the manner of an action or event.
- Adjective versus adverb is no longer presented as a simple person-or-thing
  versus action distinction. Adjectives can occur before nouns or complete a
  linking meaning, while manner adverbs describe how actions are performed.
- The previous claim that `be, look, sound`, and `feel` do not describe actions
  was removed. The same verb can link a subject to a quality or name an action:
  `The fabric feels soft`, but `Maya felt the edge carefully`.

  consonant plus `le`, and many `-ic` forms. Useful stored spellings such as
  `truly` and `fully` prevent a pattern from becoming an invention rule.

  forms match.

  after the expected time, while `lately` means recently. `Fastly` is explicitly
  excluded as the standard high-speed manner form.
- Neutral end position is now a strong default rather than an absolute law. The
  lesson keeps a short direct object beside its verb but also teaches normal
  preverb manner in `Maya carefully opened the fragile package`.
- Frequency and manner positions work together in one sentence: `Maya always

- Common informal U.S. flat forms such as `Drive safe`, `Go slow`, and `Hold

  chunk learning but states clearly that a meaning tendency cannot safely
  predict an unfamiliar verb.

  `enjoy teaching`, `ask Maya to wait`, and `look forward to meeting`.

  The second verb does not receive another tense or agreement marker, and an
  `-ing` complement does not require an added form of `be`.

  someone travels, while `on the airport bus` locates someone on a particular
  bus. Controlled items also separate `get on / off` from `get in / out of`.
- `Travel`, `trip`, and `journey` no longer receive absolute or misleading
  definitions. The page treats `travel` as a verb or usually uncountable
  activity noun, `trip` as one countable occasion, and `journey` as movement or
  experience from one place to another.

  at` with a specific place, general `get to`, and the exception `get home`.

- Travel problems now include a delay, cancellation, changed gate, missed

## Town & directions: issues found and resolved

- The former page organized language as “moving chunks” and “sitting chunks.”
  The revision distinguishes executable route instructions from location
  relationships and connects both to a traveler’s viewpoint.
- `Left` and `right` now belong to the traveler’s current facing direction. A
  route establishes a shared start point before using either side and updates
  the viewpoint after every turn.
- Directions are no longer treated as interchangeable commands. The route
  moves through six ordered jobs: establish, move, change direction, verify,
  arrive, and recover from going too far.
- Movement work separates `walk along`, `go straight`, `turn at`, `turn onto`,
  `go past`, and `cross`. Location work separates `on`, `at the end of`, `on
  your left / right`, `on the corner of`, `next to`, and `across from`.
- `Past` after `go` is distinguished from past-tense `passed`, while movement
  `cross the street` is distinguished from the relationship `across from the
  park`.
- `At` identifies an exact turn point and `onto` identifies the new street
  entered. Ordinal turn points such as `the second traffic light` prevent the
  route from depending on an unmarked turn.
- A block is no longer defined as one universal distance or simply passing one
  street. Its length and count depend on the layout and stated start point;
  approximate walking time or distance can clarify it.
- The claim that `Where is the museum?` is unsuitable for a stranger was
  removed. Both `Excuse me, where is... ?` and `Excuse me, how do I get to... ?`
  are natural; they request location and route respectively.
- Clarification language now checks distance, slower repetition, an ordinal,
  a side, a street type, or a complete step. Repeating a route is framed as
  responsible communication rather than failure.
- Pronoun reference is kept only when one landmark is available. When a bank
  and library could both be `it`, the place name is repeated.
- Landmarks now have explicit route roles: a turn point, a passed check, a
  destination relation, or a too-far signal. The learner must preserve the role
  instead of merely recognizing the place name.
